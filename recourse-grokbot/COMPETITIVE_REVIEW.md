# COMPETITIVE_REVIEW.md

Instructed to be aggressive about finding prior art and to say plainly if something kills the novelty rather than rationalize around it. Findings below are organized by category, since "does something like this already exist" collapses several genuinely different products that are easy to conflate.

**Research method caveat:** conducted via web search only (this environment's direct page-fetch tool was blocked for every domain tried — see `README.md`). Category-level claims below are reasonably confident; specific claims about any one product's current feature set should be treated as needing a live check before being repeated as fact in, e.g., a pitch deck.

## Category 1 — RAG policy chatbots ("what does the handbook say")

Real, common, mostly student/hobbyist projects: multiple GitHub repos and at least one arXiv paper (Unimib Assistant, University of Milano-Bicocca) implement retrieval-augmented Q&A over a university's own handbook/policy PDFs. Function: answer a natural-language question by retrieving and summarizing the relevant policy chunk.

**How this differs from Recourse:** these are stateless Q&A. They don't open a case, don't track a specific student's deadline against a specific decision, don't check evidence completeness, don't track the institution's own obligations, and don't persist or monitor anything over time. A RAG chatbot answering "what's the appeal deadline" and Recourse computing "your specific deadline is May 28, 1:00pm, and here's why, and I'll re-check the policy hasn't changed by then" are different classes of system, even though the first retrieval step looks similar. **Verdict: adjacent, not competitive.**

## Category 2 — AI appeal-writing tools

This is the category closest to Recourse's *subject matter* and furthest from its *design philosophy*, and the one most worth taking seriously.

- **AppealsHero** (appealshero.com) — an EdTech SaaS explicitly built for this: "AI software for academic integrity appeal letters, grade disputes, and standing, grounded in university policies." It appears to do guided intake, summarize relevant policy, and produce a draft appeal letter behind a paid unlock, with a "official policy index" per institution. This is the single closest existing product to Recourse's stated problem space.
- Multiple smaller/generic tools (Jenova's "Writing Assistant," various "AI appeal letter generator" sites, "AppealHero"-adjacent SEO content) do a thinner version of the same thing with no apparent institution-specific policy grounding.
- **Why this doesn't kill Recourse's novelty, but does narrow its claim:** every one of these tools' end product is an appeal letter draft. That is exactly the artifact Recourse refuses to produce as its primary output (`SAFETY.md` §3, §7), for a reason these tools themselves seem not to be built around: some institutions prohibit or discourage AI-authored appeal content outright, and secondary reporting (a 2026 NASFAA survey said to find that 67% of financial-aid professionals treat AI-drafted appeals as a compliance/authenticity risk) suggests reviewers are alert to it. Both of those are second-hand here and neither is verified by this project; what Recourse relies on is the narrow, checkable half — that institutions do restrict AI-authored appeal content — not any claim about how reviewers score it. AppealsHero's own marketing reportedly tells users to "verify deadlines and contacts on official university sites" themselves — i.e., even the closest competitor doesn't claim to do live-verified deadline tracking or institutional-obligation tracking; it claims to help with the letter.
- **Where Recourse must be honest about overlap:** if AppealsHero (or a similar tool) added deadline verification, evidence checklisting, and institution-obligation tracking as features wrapped around its existing letter-generation core, the "structured case logic" half of Recourse's differentiation would erode fast — that's a roadmap risk, not a solved problem. Recourse's durable differentiator is not "we also check policy" (AppealsHero claims that too) — it's the **refusal to generate narrative by default**, the **symmetric obligation model**, and **persistent unprompted monitoring**, none of which appear to be this category's design center.

## Category 3 — Institutional case-management systems

**Maxient** and **Symplicity Advocate** are the dominant products here: case management for the *institution's* conduct office, Title IX coordinator, or behavioral-intervention team — intake forms, workflow routing, templated letters, analytics, Banner/Workday integration, audit trails. **Grand River Solutions' "Case Tracker"** is a similar institution-side product specifically for Title IX/equity offices.

**How this differs from Recourse:** these are sold to and operated by the institution, for the institution's own process management. The student is a data subject in these systems, not the user. Recourse is explicitly the mirror image — a tool the *student* owns, that has no institutional access or integration at all. There is no overlap in user, access model, or deployment target. **Verdict: not competitive; adjacent market on the opposite side of the table.**

## Category 4 — Human ombuds / advising services

**AdvocatED** (getadvocated.com) is the clearest example: a paid, human-staffed practice that represents students through academic misconduct, conduct hearings, Title IX, and dismissal appeals inside the institution's own process — including ATIXA-certified Title IX hearing advisors. University ombuds offices (referenced directly in the Kent State research, e.g. the Kent State Office of the Student Ombuds) do a lower-intensity, free, informal version of similar guidance.

**How this differs from Recourse:** these are human labor, priced per case or per hour, with the professional judgment (and liability) of a human advisor behind every recommendation. They can exercise judgment calls (e.g., "this is worth appealing even though it's a close call") that Recourse deliberately does not — Recourse fails closed exactly where a human advisor would use discretion. **Verdict: not competitive on delivery model; potentially complementary** — a plausible real product shape is Recourse doing the structured triage/tracking work for free or cheap, with a clear handoff to a human advisor (an AdvocatED-like service, or a campus ombuds office) for cases needing actual judgment or representation, which Recourse is explicitly not designed to provide.

## Category 5 — "Policy as code" / "Rules as Code" research

A real, active research area: government "Rules as Code" initiatives (cited via UNSW's Allens Hub, The Conversation, and multiple arXiv preprints on "Everything as Code" and compliance-as-code) aim to represent legislation and regulation as machine-executable logic, generally for government rulemaking or corporate regulatory compliance (RegTech). At least one 2025-era arXiv preprint on "Law-as-Code" describes extracting executable rules from legislation and validating with real-world case studies.

**How this differs from Recourse:** this research area is almost entirely about the *supply side* — governments or compliance teams encoding rules for systems to check against — not about an individual rights-holder's own procedural situation, and not about student-facing, appeal-specific, dual-obligation, persistently-monitored case tracking. Recourse's `POLICY_IR.md` is a narrow, purpose-built instance of the same general idea ("compile a natural-language rule into structured, checkable logic") applied to a domain (student appeal rights) this literature does not appear to specifically target. **Verdict: closest academic/conceptual cousin, not a competing product** — worth citing as intellectual grounding, not worth treating as prior art that's "already done this."

## Honest overall verdict

Nothing found is *the same product* as Recourse — student-owned, refuses-to-author, dual-obligation, policy-as-code, persistently-monitored. The category that comes closest, and the one to genuinely worry about, is **Category 2 (AI appeal writers, specifically AppealsHero)**: same target user, same subject matter, existing market presence and pricing model, multi-institution policy coverage already built. It has not (as far as this search could establish) built the structured obligation-tracking and default-non-authoring architecture that is Recourse's actual thesis — but it is the one competitor for whom "they'll just add that" is a plausible, not hypothetical, threat. If asked to bet, the honest answer is: **Recourse's idea is not already built, but its most dangerous competitor already has distribution and a two-sided head start on the "boring" half of the problem (policy lookup) that this project is treating as its differentiator's supporting infrastructure rather than its core claim.** The core claim — refuse to author, track both parties, fail closed, monitor persistently — is not something this search found anyone doing for this domain.
